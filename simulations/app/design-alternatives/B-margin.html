Risk ratio to odds ratio

study 05 · argument rr_to_or · 15 conditions · 1,000 replications each

Converting a reported risk ratio to an odds ratio requires a baseline risk the source study usually does not give. This study varies the true baseline risk and the value assumed in its place, and asks what the substitution costs.

What the current view shows

Mean root mean squared error is smallest for dipietrantonj (0.903) and largest for vanderweele_rr_squared (1.753). The remaining three routes fall between them, within 0.017 of one another — a gap this view cannot test.1

The ordering by RMSE and the ordering by bias disagree. vanderweele_rr_squared has the smallest mean absolute bias of the five (0.292) alongside the largest RMSE, because its replication-to-replication variability is roughly twice that of the others (empirical SE 1.696 against 0.759 to 0.852). Closer on average, much less dependable on any one study.

Every route reports a model standard error near twice the empirical one and covers the population parameter 99.2% to 100% of the time against a nominal 95%.2 These intervals are too wide rather than well calibrated.

This slice holds the misspecification at zero

The assumed baseline risk equals the true one (br_guess = br = 0.01), so nothing in the current view is misspecified — the effect this study exists to measure is switched off.

0.01 is also the smallest baseline risk in the grid. At n = 50 the whole 2×2 table holds between 0.31 and 0.75 expected cases, so under the generating model the Haldane–Anscombe correction applies to 91%–99% of replications there, and to 26%–76% at n = 300. Much of what is compared here is the behaviour of that correction.

Both follow from the default selection, not from the data.

1 · No Monte Carlo error for RMSE

These aggregates record a Monte Carlo standard error for bias, empirical SE, the SE ratio and coverage, but not for RMSE. Differences between the three middle routes therefore cannot be separated from simulation noise on this measure. Switch to bias or coverage to get an error bar and a resolution band.

2 · Over-coverage is not calibration

SE ratio runs 1.77 to 2.27 here and mean interval width from 5.01 to 12.87 on the log odds-ratio scale. Coverage near 1.00 reflects intervals about twice as wide as they should be.

The programme records an over-wide standard error across both binary-outcome studies, including transpose, which only copies the log odds-ratio standard error through. It names the continuity correction as one candidate and has not chased it. The correction rates above are consistent with that candidate but do not establish it.

Outside this view

No replication was non-estimable in this slice. Across the whole of study 05, grant fails to return an estimate in up to 62% of a cell, where rr × br_guess ≥ 1. That region is not reachable from the current selection.

Reading the measures

Bias describes the point estimate. RMSE adds the estimator’s variability. The SE ratio asks whether the reported standard error matches the real one. Coverage is the only measure that degrades under either a wrong estimate or a wrong standard error, which is why the table prints all of them and the selected measure only sets the order.
